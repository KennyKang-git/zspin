## **§7.1 The strong question, stated precisely**

Weak sufficiency (§5) establishes that *a* definite outcome occurs and that the ensemble of outcomes is Born-distributed. The strong question asks something more: given the complete physical specification available before the run, is *which* outcome occurs determined, or reducible to a fuller deterministic description? In quantum-trajectory language, the conditional state evolves by the stochastic master equation driven by the measurement innovation (a Wiener process in the diffusive limit, a counting process in the jump limit). The outcome on a given run is fixed by the realized innovation path. The strong question is therefore: is the innovation path eliminable — for instance, fixed by a hidden variable, by fuller knowledge of the initial state, or by a Z-Spin-internal deterministic substrate?

## **§7.2 The estimation-stability theorem**

The decisive structural input is the *estimation-stability* (or filter-stability) property of non-demolition quantum trajectories, proved by Benoist–Pellegrini \[4\] and in the discrete setting by Bauer–Benoist–Bernard \[3\] (see also \[7\]). Informally: two observers who process the *same* measurement record but start from *different* prior states converge to the same conditional state. Formally, let ρ^(t) and ρ̃^(t) be two solutions of the same QND stochastic master equation driven by the same realized record, with ρ^(0) ≠ ρ̃^(0) (the second having full support). Under the standard non-demolition and irreducibility hypotheses, ‖ρ^(t) − ρ̃^(t)‖ → 0 almost surely, exponentially fast. The limit is the *same random pure state* for both, namely the eigenstate selected by the record.

The interpretive content is sharp: the selected outcome is a **functional of the record**, not of the prior. The prior state influences only the *statistics* of which records occur (Born), not the outcome that any given record produces. Two observers who disagree completely about the initial quantum state nevertheless agree on the outcome once they share the record.

## **§7.3 Monte-Carlo confirmation**

We confirmed estimation stability directly (§8, Block 3b; zs\_q16\_outcome\_mc\_v1\_0.py). For a binary QND measurement via a dim-2 probe, a single record was generated per run and fed to three filters with priors (i) the true Born prior, (ii) an ignorant uniform prior, and (iii) a deliberately wrong prior \[0.05, 0.95\]. In **2000 / 2000 \= 100%** of runs all three filters converged to the *same* outcome — the one selected by the shared record — with the prior-A and prior-B filters agreeing (L₁ \< 10⁻³) after on average 12.8 transits. Independently (Block 3a), fixing the initial state and varying only the noise reproduced the Born frequencies (e.g. \[0.6038, 0.3962\] against Born \[0.6, 0.4\]): different records give different outcomes. Together these establish empirically that the per-run randomness lives entirely in the record.

## **§7.4 The residual is non-epistemic**

The combination of §7.2 and §7.3 yields the principal negative result of this paper. The strong-sufficiency residual — the irreducibility of “which outcome” — is **non-epistemic**: it is not a deficit of knowledge about the quantum state that a better description could remove. The randomness is intrinsic to the record, i.e. to the measurement interaction itself, not to our ignorance of the system.
