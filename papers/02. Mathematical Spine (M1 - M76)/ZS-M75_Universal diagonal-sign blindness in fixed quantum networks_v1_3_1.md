## Abstract

We characterize when the sign of a diagonal potential is invisible to configuration populations in a finite quantum network with fixed complex hopping amplitudes. For a zero-diagonal Hermitian matrix $K$, the transition probabilities generated by $K+V$ and $K-V$ agree for every real diagonal $V$ and every time if and only if $K=-g\overline K g^\dagger$ for a diagonal unitary $g$. This is equivalent to balance of the edge gains $-K_{ba}/\overline{K_{ba}}$. The necessity proof isolates an induced unbalanced cycle by large but finite exterior detuning and uses an explicit cycle-interference coefficient. It requires neither independent edge parameters nor positive weights or spectral nondegeneracy.

For several fixed hopping modes, the analogous universal equality under all finite coherent switching experiments holds exactly when the same diagonal unitary works for every mode. Unbalanced parallel edges produce a cubic witness; otherwise a positive convex mixture and a positive-duration product formula reduce the problem to the single-mode theorem. A walk expansion supplies a lower bound on witness order in terms of the shortest unbalanced closed walk.

For a fixed potential, we prove a sharp local return law: a nonzero single-site potential is invisible in its return probability exactly when the local spectral measure is symmetric, and the first nonzero odd moment otherwise determines the leading contrast. The blind-potential set is real algebraic and proper for an unbalanced network. On an unbalanced bare cycle it is exactly the scalar line, except on the quadrilateral, where it is precisely the two-dimensional space of alternating potentials; every visible potential has first contrast degree one more than the cycle length.

For arbitrary complex bipartite hopping and a sublattice-staggered potential, cross-sublattice populations are always blind. We prove that complete blindness at any nonzero potential strength is equivalent to entrywise phase alignment of the spectral projectors of both Gram blocks, or equivalently of finitely many Gram powers. This criterion is independent of potential strength and remains valid at coincident spectral gaps. A vanishing-Wronskian argument removes the non-resonance restriction of the earlier spectral test, and gives a sharp within-part response law, of seventh order when the first two Gram powers are nonaligned at the observed entry. The quadrilateral and dense unitary coupling blocks supply time-symmetric families with flux and nonzero on-site terms beyond the familiar gauge-real and zero-diagonal bipartite sufficient families. Exact finite checks accompany the proofs. Controls are mathematical inputs; no action-derived physical instrument is asserted.

## 1. Question and relation to previous work

Configuration measurements discard amplitudes and retain their squared moduli. Consequently, inequivalent Hamiltonian matrices can give identical population data. A diagonal change of basis always preserves these data, and a suitable combination of complex conjugation and sign reversal does so as well. The converse is more delicate: equality for a particular pair of Hamiltonians does not, in general, force a diagonal gauge relation.

Here the hopping matrices are fixed, but the diagonal perturbation ranges over all real diagonal matrices. This quantifier is the essential additional information. We ask whether

$$
 |(e^{-it(K+V)})_{ba}|^2=|(e^{-it(K-V)})_{ba}|^2
 \quad\text{for every real diagonal }V,\ a,b\in\{1,\ldots,N\},\ t\geq0. \tag{1.1}
$$

For multiple modes, the same $V$ is used throughout each experiment and the mode sequence and positive durations may vary. Arbitrarily changing $V$ during a single experiment is not required. A fixed $V=cI$ is always invisible, even on an unbalanced network; it therefore cannot be substituted for the universal quantifier in (1.1). Sections 6.2 and 8 then turn to fixed potentials, where the universal quantifier is dropped and the question becomes spectral and symmetry-driven.

## 2. Model and gain balance

Let $\mathcal H=\mathbb C^N$ with a fixed configuration basis $\{|a\rangle\}_{a=1}^N$. Set $\hbar=1$. For $j=1,\ldots,J$, let $K_j$ be a fixed Hermitian matrix with zero diagonal, and let

$$
 H_{z,j}=K_j+zV,\qquad V=\operatorname{diag}(v_1,\ldots,v_N),\quad v_a\in\mathbb R,\quad z\in\{+1,-1\}. \tag{2.1}
$$

A scalar branch-even diagonal in each mode can be removed as an overall phase. Nonconstant branch-even diagonals are excluded from the classification; Section 7 states their separate quartic response.

For a word $w=(j_1,\ldots,j_m)$ with $t_r>0$, define

$$
 U_z(w,\mathbf t;V)=e^{-it_mH_{z,j_m}}\cdots e^{-it_1H_{z,j_1}},\qquad
 \Delta M_{ba}=|(U_+)_{ba}|^2-|(U_-)_{ba}|^2. \tag{2.2}
$$

All-population equality means equality for every input configuration and output configuration. It also covers every diagonal initial density matrix and every diagonal effect by linearity. It does not assert equality for arbitrary coherent preparations or arbitrary observables. The preparation, basis, word, and readout are the same on the two branches.

The **mode multigraph** has one undirected labelled edge $(a,b,j)$ whenever $(K_j)_{ba}\ne0$. A pair of modes on the same unordered pair of vertices creates parallel edges. Orient an edge from $a$ to $b$ and define

$$
 r_{j,ba}=-\frac{(K_j)_{ba}}{\overline{(K_j)_{ba}}},\qquad
 h(\gamma)=\prod_{e\in\gamma}(K_{j_e})_{b_ea_e}. \tag{2.3}
$$

The reverse gain is the reciprocal. For a closed walk of hopping length $\ell$,

$$
 r(\gamma)=(-1)^\ell\frac{h(\gamma)}{\overline{h(\gamma)}}.
 \tag{2.4}
$$

The graph is **balanced** when every closed walk has gain one. Equivalently, odd closed walks have $\operatorname{Re}h=0$, and even closed walks have $\operatorname{Im}h=0$. This balance concerns the transformed gains $r$, not the original hopping phases themselves.

**Lemma 2.1 (standard gain criterion, specialized).** The following are equivalent:

1. The mode multigraph is balanced.
2. There is a single diagonal unitary $g$ such that $K_j=-g\overline{K_j}g^\dagger$ for every $j$.
3. There is a single diagonal unitary $q$ and real skew-symmetric matrices $R_j$ such that $K_j=q(iR_j)q^\dagger$ for every $j$.

**Proof.** The entrywise condition in item 2 is $g_b/g_a=r_{j,ba}$. Set a root phase in each component and propagate phases on a spanning forest. The remaining edges are consistent precisely when their fundamental-cycle gains are one. This is the standard cycle-switching argument [R1], with parallel edges retained. If $g=qq^{\mathsf T}$, obtained by taking a square root of each diagonal phase, then $q^\dagger K_jq=-\overline{q^\dagger K_jq}$. Hermiticity makes this matrix $i$ times a real skew-symmetric matrix. The converse follows by direct substitution. □

**Corollary 2.2 (sufficiency under switching).** Balance implies $\Delta M=0$ for all $V,w,\mathbf t$.

**Proof.** The same $g$ gives

$$
 H_{-,j}=-g\overline{H_{+,j}}g^\dagger,\qquad
 e^{-itH_{-,j}}=g\overline{e^{-itH_{+,j}}}g^\dagger.
$$

Complex conjugation preserves the order of a product. The intermediate $g^\dagger g$ factors cancel, so $U_-=g\overline{U_+}g^\dagger$, which preserves every entry modulus. No pulse reversal or matrix transpose is inserted. □

Disconnected components are treated separately; isolated vertices are blind. In exact arithmetic the spanning-forest test uses $O(N+E_{\rm multi})$ edge operations. This count is not a bit-complexity estimate for arbitrary symbolic input. Floating near-balance requires an error analysis; a tolerance-based graph label is not an exact certificate.


## 3. Walk interference and an order barrier

**Theorem 3.1 (holonomy selection and girth barrier).** In the multivariate Taylor expansion of (2.2), each branch-odd coefficient is a real-polynomial combination of

$$
 \operatorname{Re}h(\gamma)\quad(|\gamma|\text{ odd}),\qquad
 \operatorname{Im}h(\gamma)\quad(|\gamma|\text{ even}). \tag{3.1}
$$

The polynomials contain an odd total number of diagonal-potential insertions. If $\ell_*$ is the length of the shortest unbalanced closed walk, every coefficient of total time degree at most $\ell_*$ vanishes, for every word and every $V$. If no unbalanced walk exists, all coefficients vanish.

**Proof.** Expand each pulse exponential in words of hopping factors and diagonal insertions. A term with $n$ hops and $m$ insertions has phase $(-i)^{n+m}$, a real duration/factorial coefficient, a hopping product $P$, and a real monomial in $v$. Pair two amplitude terms in the population, with counts $(n_1,m_1)$ and $(n_2,m_2)$. The hopping product is $h=\overline{P_1}P_2$, the holonomy of the second path followed by the reverse of the first. Write $\ell=n_1+n_2$ and $m=m_1+m_2$. The phase is

$$
 i^{n_1+m_1}(-i)^{n_2+m_2}=(-1)^{n_2+m_2}i^{\ell+m}.
$$

Branch subtraction retains exactly odd $m$. After pairing complex conjugate terms, odd $\ell$ selects $\operatorname{Re}h$, and even $\ell$ selects $\operatorname{Im}h$, with real signs absorbed into the coefficients. For total degree $d\le\ell_*$, an odd $m\ge1$ leaves $\ell\le d-1<\ell_*$; that closed walk is balanced and its selected component vanishes. Finite-dimensional exponentials are entire, so coefficientwise vanishing gives the asserted equality. □

The barrier is a lower bound. A chosen $V$, input/output pair, or word can cancel its first eligible coefficient. Nor does the theorem assert that one universal pulse count or signal size works for every unbalanced graph.

### 3.1 An isolated cycle witness

**Lemma 3.2 (cycle coefficient).** Let $K$ be supported on one simple cycle $C$ of length $\ell\ge3$, with nonzero edge weights and zero diagonal. Choose distinct vertices $a,b$. Let $w_1,w_2:a\to b$ be the two simple arcs, with lengths $n_1+n_2=\ell$. Set

$$
 h=\overline{P(w_1)}P(w_2),\qquad S_i=\sum_{x\in w_i}v_x,
$$

where the endpoints occur in each arc sum. Then the coefficient at degree $\ell+1$ is

$$
 c_{\ell+1}=4\operatorname{Re}\!\left\{
 (-i)i^{n_1}(-i)^{n_2}h
 \left[\frac{S_2}{n_1!(n_2+1)!}-\frac{S_1}{(n_1+1)!n_2!}\right]\right\}. \tag{3.2}
$$

All lower branch-contrast coefficients vanish. For adjacent $a,b$, take $n_1=1$, and choose $V=P_c$ with $c\ne a,b$ on the cycle. Then

$$
 c_{\ell+1}=\frac{4}{\ell!}\operatorname{Re}\{(-i)^{\ell-1}h\}. \tag{3.3}
$$

This coefficient is nonzero exactly when the cycle is unbalanced.

**Proof.** Closed walks shorter than $\ell$ on a bare cycle reduce to backtracks and are balanced. At degree $\ell+1$, terms with three or more potential insertions have fewer than $\ell$ hops and vanish by Theorem 3.1. The only unbalanced hop contribution is the pair of complementary arcs. The zero-insertion amplitude for an arc of length $n$ is $(-i)^nt^nP/n!$. Its one-insertion amplitude, summed over its $n+1$ possible insertion positions, is $(-i)^{n+1}t^{n+1}zSP/(n+1)!$. Pairing these two possibilities gives (3.2): one factor two comes from the conjugate pair and one from branch subtraction. With adjacent endpoints and the stated $P_c$, $S_1=0,S_2=1$, giving (3.3). For odd $\ell$ the displayed real part is a nonzero real sign times $\operatorname{Re}h$; for even $\ell$ it is a nonzero real sign times $\operatorname{Im}h$. □

The cycle statement can also be applied when it is the unique shortest relevant unbalanced cycle and all competing walks at that degree are balanced. The bare-cycle form above is sufficient for the central necessity proof and avoids imposing a local simple-cycle condition where a rooted closed walk is needed.


## 4. Complete classification for a fixed hopping matrix

**Theorem 4.1 (universal diagonal-sign classification).** For any fixed zero-diagonal Hermitian $K\in\mathbb C^{N\times N}$, the following are equivalent:

1. Equation (1.1) holds for every real diagonal $V$.
2. Every cycle in the support graph has doubled gain one.
3. $K=-g\overline Kg^\dagger$ for a diagonal unitary $g$.

In particular, if these conditions fail, there exist a finite real diagonal $V$, two configurations, and a finite positive time giving a nonzero population contrast. The edge weights may be tied, signed, or complex. No spectral assumption is needed.

The missing step beyond Lemma 2.1 is necessity of balance from population equality. We prove it by a quantitative isolation lemma.

**Lemma 4.2 (exterior detuning).** Let

$$
 H_z(R)=\begin{pmatrix}A_z&B\\B^\dagger&C+zRI\end{pmatrix},\qquad R>0,
 \tag{4.1}
$$

where $A_z,C$ are Hermitian. Let $P$ project onto the first block and write $b=\|B\|$. For $t\ge0$,

$$
 \big\|Pe^{-itH_z(R)}P\big|_{P\mathcal H}-e^{-itA_z}\big\|
 \le \eta_z(t,R):=\frac{b^2}{R}
 \left[2t+\frac{\|C\|+\|A_z\|+b}{2}t^2\right]. \tag{4.2}
$$

Consequently, the error in any within-block population is at most $2\eta_z$, and the error in a branch contrast is at most $2(\eta_++\eta_-)$.

**Proof.** Evolve a unit vector initially in the first block and write its components as $x(t),y(t)$. Their norms are at most one and $y(0)=0$. Variation of constants gives

$$
 y(t)=-i e^{-it(C+zRI)}\int_0^t e^{izRs}e^{iCs}B^\dagger x(s)\,ds.
$$

For $F(s)=e^{iCs}B^\dagger x(s)$, we have $\|F(s)\|\le b$. Since $x'=-iA_zx-iBy$,

$$
 \|F'(s)\|\le b(\|C\|+\|A_z\|+b).
$$

Integration by parts against $e^{izRs}$ therefore yields

$$
 \|y(s)\|\le\frac{b}{R}[2+s(\|C\|+\|A_z\|+b)].
$$

Using $x(t)-e^{-itA_z}x(0)=-i\int_0^t e^{-i(t-s)A_z}By(s)ds$ proves (4.2), uniformly over initial unit vectors. Finally, for matrix elements of contractions $u,v$, $\big||u|^2-|v|^2\big|\le2|u-v|$. □

**Proof of Theorem 4.1.** Balance implies the gauge and population equality by Section 2. For the converse, suppose the support is unbalanced. A shortest unbalanced closed walk contains no repeated vertex other than its endpoints: splitting at a repeated vertex would express its gain as a product of gains of shorter closed walks. It is therefore a simple cycle. It also has no chord. A chord would split it into two shorter cycles whose gains multiply to its gain; if both were balanced, the original would be balanced. Thus a shortest unbalanced cycle $C_0$ is an induced cycle.

Let $P$ project onto its vertices. The principal matrix $K_{C_0}=PKP|_{P\mathcal H}$ is supported on that bare cycle. Choose adjacent vertices $a,b$ and an interior vertex $c\ne a,b$, and put $W=P_c$ on the cycle. By Lemma 3.2, $K_{C_0}\pm W$ have nonzero population contrast at sufficiently small positive time $t_0$. Denote that contrast by $\delta_0\ne0$.

Order the vertices with the cycle first and choose

$$
 V_R=W\oplus RI_{C_0^c}.
 \tag{4.3}
$$

Then $K\pm V_R$ have the form (4.1), with $A_\pm=K_{C_0}\pm W$, $C=K_{C_0^c}$, and the original, unchanged exterior couplings $B$. Equation (4.2) implies convergence of both within-cycle population laws to the isolated-cycle laws at the same $t_0$. For a sufficiently large **finite** $R$, their contrast differs from $\delta_0$ by less than $|\delta_0|/2$, so it is nonzero. This contradicts (1.1). If there is no exterior block, the isolated-cycle witness already suffices. □

This construction keeps every hopping entry fixed. Exterior cancellation is removed by varying allowed diagonal entries, not by pretending that tied edge variables can be varied independently.

### 4.1 Finite resource choices and limitations

For an isolated cycle coefficient $c_q\ne0$, $q=\ell+1$, let $\kappa=\max_z\|K_{C_0}+zW\|$. The density generator has trace-norm bound $2\kappa$. A convenient Taylor estimate is

$$
 |\Delta M_{ba}(t)-c_qt^q|
 \le \frac{2e^{2\kappa t}(2\kappa t)^{q+1}}{(q+1)!}. \tag{4.4}
$$

Thus one may choose

$$
 0<t_0\le\min\left\{\frac1{2\kappa},\frac{|c_q|(q+1)!}{4e(2\kappa)^{q+1}}\right\},
 \tag{4.5}
$$

giving $|\delta_0|\ge |c_q|t_0^q/2$. Define

$$
 F(t)=b^2\left[2t+\frac{\|C\|+\max_z\|A_z\|+b}{2}t^2\right].
$$

Taking $R>8F(t_0)/|\delta_0|$ ensures the detuned contrast retains at least half the isolated contrast. These bounds are conservative existence certificates. They give no uniform laboratory sensitivity, bounded detuning budget, or favorable scaling with graph size. Replacing the all-$V$ domain by a specified finite set of physically allowed potentials is a different problem.

**Corollary 4.3 (arbitrary signed real weights).** For a real symmetric zero-diagonal $K$, universal diagonal-sign blindness holds if and only if its support is bipartite.

**Proof.** Every nonzero real edge has $r=-1$, independently of its sign or magnitude. Balance is therefore equivalent to every cycle having even length. Apply Theorem 4.1. □

This removes the same-sign restriction in the seed's universal classification. It does not remove possible cancellations in a particular local coefficient.


## 5. Complete classification under coherent switching

**Theorem 5.1 (common-mode universal classification).** For fixed zero-diagonal Hermitian matrices $K_1,\ldots,K_J$, the following are equivalent:

1. Every finite word of strictly positive durations is population blind for every common real diagonal $V$.
2. The mode multigraph is balanced, including all parallel-edge cycles.
3. A single diagonal unitary $g$ satisfies $K_j=-g\overline{K_j}g^\dagger$ for every $j$.

**Proof.** Sufficiency and the equivalence of items 2 and 3 were proved in Section 2. Suppose the multigraph is unbalanced.

First suppose an unbalanced two-cycle is formed by modes $p,q$ on $a,b$. With $k_j=(K_j)_{ba}$, this means $\operatorname{Im}(\overline{k_p}k_q)\ne0$. Direct expansion of two equal-duration pulses gives

$$
 [\tau^3]\Delta M_{ba}
 =4\operatorname{Im}(\overline{k_p}k_q)(v_a-v_b).
 \tag{5.1}
$$

Choose $v_a\ne v_b$. The lower branch-contrast coefficients vanish, and a sufficiently small positive $\tau$ gives a witness. The formula is derived again in Section 7.

Now suppose every two-cycle is balanced. On each edge $e=(a,b)$ in the union support, all nonzero mode entries have one common phase up to a real sign. Write

$$
 (K_j)_{ba}=\kappa_e u_{je},\qquad u_{je}\in\mathbb R,
 \tag{5.2}
$$

with $u_{je}=0$ when that mode lacks the edge. Choose $\alpha_j>0$, $\sum_j\alpha_j=1$, outside the finite union of hyperplanes

$$
 \sum_j\alpha_j u_{je}=0. \tag{5.3}
$$

Each excluded set is proper in the simplex: the coefficient vector on an existing edge is not identically zero, and a linear form that vanishes on the full simplex would have all coefficients zero. A finite union of such sets cannot cover its open interior. The mixture

$$
 K_\alpha=\sum_j\alpha_jK_j
 \tag{5.4}
$$

has the entire union support and exactly the same doubled edge gains. Because parallel gains agree, unbalance of the multigraph implies unbalance of this ordinary support graph. Theorem 4.1 supplies $V,a,b,t>0$ for which $K_\alpha\pm V$ have nonzero contrast.

For that single, fixed $V$, the finite-dimensional product formula gives

$$
 \left[e^{-i\alpha_Jt(K_J+zV)/n}\cdots e^{-i\alpha_1t(K_1+zV)/n}\right]^n
 \longrightarrow e^{-it(K_\alpha+zV)}.
 \tag{5.5}
$$

Every factor has positive duration, and the same $V$ appears in every pulse. Convergence for both signs and continuity of populations imply that some finite $n$ retains the nonzero contrast. This violates item 1. □

No negative control time, independently adjustable sign of a hopping mode, or independently switchable diagonal pulse is used. A naive equal-weight average can delete an edge, which is why the hyperplane exclusion in (5.3) is necessary.

### 5.1 Quantitative product-formula control

Let $H_{z,j}=K_j+zV$ and

$$
 C_z=\sum_{p<q}\alpha_p\alpha_q\|[H_{z,p},H_{z,q}]\|.
$$

For the left side $T_{z,n}(t)$ of (5.5),

$$
 \|T_{z,n}(t)-e^{-it(K_\alpha+zV)}\|\le\frac{t^2C_z}{2n}.
 \tag{5.6}
$$

To see this, the two-factor product error follows by a double Duhamel integral bounded by $s^2\|[A,B]\|/2$ for anti-Hermitian generators. Apply it successively to one $J$-factor step, bound each commutator with the partial sum by the sum of pairwise norms, and telescope $n$ unitary steps. Equation (5.6) follows with $s=t/n$.

The error in the branch contrast is at most $t^2(C_++C_-)/n$. If the effective static contrast is $\delta\ne0$, any integer $n>2t^2(C_++C_-)/|\delta|$ keeps at least half its size. This supplies a finite witness budget once a static witness is chosen; it is not a claim of optimal pulse count.

### 5.2 A genuinely collective mode example

On three vertices, let the three real modes carry only the edges $01,12,20$, respectively, each with unit weight. Each mode separately is universally blind. Their union is a triangle, and their equally weighted mixture is unbalanced. For $V=\operatorname{diag}(0,0,1)$, total time $t=0.8$, and transition $0\to1$, the effective static contrast is approximately (-0.00938209646). The cyclic product in (5.5) with $n=2$, six pulses in total, has contrast approximately (-0.00476449552). One repetition of this particular word has exactly zero contrast: each pulse acts on one edge, the $0\to1$ amplitude factors through the return amplitude of the two-site pulse on edge $12$ at site $1$, and a two-site return probability is even in the potential on the other site. This illustrates why the theorem promises existence of a finite word rather than visibility of every word. Row V02 checks these values and the operator error bound.


## 6. Return probabilities and rooted odd walks

The preceding theorems use a selected transition between distinct sites. Local return probabilities require a different geometric invariant.

Let $K$ be real symmetric with zero diagonal. Define $\gamma_x$ as the length of the shortest odd closed walk in the nonzero support of $K$ that starts and ends at $x$. Set $\gamma_x=\infty$ if there is none. This is a support invariant and gives a lower bound on the first visible order. The sharp order is governed by the first nonzero odd matrix moment, which can sit strictly above $\gamma_x$ when signed or complex walk weights cancel; Theorem 6.2 below makes this exact for arbitrary Hermitian $K$.

**Theorem 6.1 (corrected rooted return law).** For $V=\chi P_x$, $\chi\in\mathbb R$, and finite $\gamma_x$, all coefficients of $\Delta M_{xx}(t)$ below degree $\gamma_x+1$ vanish, and

$$
 [t^{\gamma_x+1}]\Delta M_{xx}
 =\frac{4(-1)^{(\gamma_x+1)/2}(1-\gamma_x)}{(\gamma_x+1)!}
 (K^{\gamma_x})_{xx}\chi.
 \tag{6.1}
$$

The right side may be zero for signed weights. If $\gamma_x=\infty$, the connected component of $x$ is bipartite and the return contrast vanishes at all times.

**Proof.** For a real symmetric Hamiltonian, each population is even in $t$. In a branch-odd term of even time degree, the total number of potential insertions is odd, so the total hop count is odd. A pair of closed walks rooted at $x$ with odd total length must contain an odd rooted closed walk. Its length is at least $\gamma_x$. This proves vanishing below $\gamma_x+1$.

At degree $\gamma_x+1$, there are exactly $\gamma_x$ hops and one potential insertion. The two rooted hop walks must have lengths $\gamma_x$ and zero: otherwise the odd one would be shorter than $\gamma_x$. A shortest odd rooted walk has no internal revisit to $x$, because splitting there gives two shorter rooted closed walks, one odd. Since $V$ is supported only at $x$, the long walk has exactly two allowed insertion slots, at its beginning and end.

Let $A=(K^{\gamma_x})_{xx}$, the sum of all these minimal odd walk weights. The relevant amplitude coefficients are $1$, $-iz\chi$, $(-i)^{\gamma_x}A/\gamma_x!$, and $2z\chi(-i)^{\gamma_x+1}A/(\gamma_x+1)!$. The two cross terms in the branch difference give

$$
 4\chi\operatorname{Re}[(-i)^{\gamma_x+1}]A
 \left[\frac2{(\gamma_x+1)!}-\frac1{\gamma_x!}\right],
$$

which is (6.1). If no odd rooted walk exists, an odd cycle anywhere in the connected component would create one by going to that cycle and returning along the same path. The component is therefore bipartite and Corollary 4.3 applies. □

### 6.1 The seed's counterexample and its repair

Take unit real weights on the edges $01,12,23,31$, and observe $x=0$. The root is a leaf and lies on no simple cycle. Nevertheless, it can traverse the triangle through its neighbor and return, so $\gamma_0=5$ and $(K^5)_{00}=2$. For $V=P_0$, exact expansion gives

$$
 \Delta M_{00}(t)=\frac2{45}t^6-\frac{29}{1260}t^8+O(t^{10}). \tag{6.2}
$$

A counterexample with a finite competing simple-cycle length is obtained by putting $x=0$ on the unit cycle $0,1,\ldots,6,0$, adding the edge $07$, and adding the triangle $7,8,9,7$. The shortest odd simple cycle through $0$ has length seven, but $\gamma_0=5$. Its return contrast begins with $2t^6/45$, contradicting a rule that forbids all terms through degree seven. Row C02 computes both examples independently of the seed's amplitude routine.

For same-sign nonzero real weights, $(K^{\gamma_x})_{xx}$ cannot cancel: each minimal odd walk has the same sign. If $\chi\ne0$, the first local return order is therefore exactly $\gamma_x+1$. For mixed signs or complex weights the first order is given exactly by Theorem 6.2. A global shortest odd cycle still gives the seed's same-sign visibility result, so the local error does not destroy that restricted conclusion.


### 6.2 The sharp local return law

Theorem 6.1 bounds the first visible order from below by a support invariant. The exact order and the exact question of when a single-site potential is invisible in its own return probability are answered by the local spectral measure. Write $m_n=(K^n)_{xx}$, which is real for Hermitian $K$, and let $\mu_x$ be the spectral measure of $K$ at $|x\rangle$, so that $m_n=\int\lambda^n\,d\mu_x$.

**Theorem 6.2 (sharp return law, arbitrary Hermitian $K$).** Let $K$ be zero-diagonal Hermitian, $x$ a site, and $V=\chi P_x$ with $\chi\in\mathbb R$.

1. If $m_n=0$ for every odd $n$, then $\Delta M_{xx}(t)=0$ for every $\chi$ and every $t$.
2. Otherwise let $2k+1$ be the least odd $n$ with $m_n\ne0$ (so $k\ge1$ because $K_{xx}=0$). Then $[t^d]\Delta M_{xx}=0$ for $d<2k+2$ and
$$
 [t^{2k+2}]\Delta M_{xx}=\frac{8k(-1)^k}{(2k+2)!}\,m_{2k+1}\,\chi ,
 \tag{6.3}
$$
which is exactly linear in $\chi$. In particular the return probability at $x$ distinguishes the sign of $\chi P_x$ for **every** $\chi\ne0$.
3. Using the same support definition of $\gamma_x$ for complex Hermitian $K$, $2k+1\ge\gamma_x$, with equality whenever the minimal odd rooted walks do not cancel, for instance for same-sign real weights; then (6.3) coincides with (6.1).

The odd moments vanish exactly when $\mu_x$ is symmetric about $0$, equivalently when the Jacobi (Lanczos) matrix of $K$ generated from $|x\rangle$ has zero diagonal. This equivalence is classical [R9].

**Proof.** Let $\mathcal K$ be the Krylov space generated by $|x\rangle$ under $K$. Since $P_x|x\rangle=|x\rangle$, the operators $K\pm\chi P_x$ leave $\mathcal K$ invariant and have the same Krylov space. In the Lanczos basis $e_0=|x\rangle,e_1,\ldots$ the restriction of $K$ is a real symmetric tridiagonal matrix $J$ with diagonal $(0,\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots)$ and positive off-diagonal entries $\beta_j=J_{j-1,j}=J_{j,j-1}$ for $j=1,\ldots,\dim\mathcal K-1$, and $P_x$ becomes $P_0$. Hence
$$
 \langle x|e^{-it(K\pm\chi P_x)}|x\rangle=\langle e_0|e^{-it(J\pm\chi P_0)}|e_0\rangle,\qquad (J^n)_{00}=m_n .
$$
The return problem is therefore the return problem of a real weighted path with a real branch-even diagonal $\operatorname{diag}(0,\alpha_1,\ldots)$ and the branch-odd potential $\chi P_0$ at its end.

Item 1. When $\alpha_0=\cdots=\alpha_{j-1}=0$, the only odd closed walk of length $2j+1$ at $0$ is the out-and-back path with one loop at site $j$, so $m_{2j+1}=\alpha_j\prod_{i\le j}\beta_i^2$. If all odd moments vanish this recursion gives $\alpha_j=0$ for every $j$ (the classical symmetry criterion [R9]), so $J$ is a real zero-diagonal path. A path is bipartite, and Corollary 4.3 gives blindness for every real diagonal, in particular for $\chi P_0$.

Item 2. Expand $\langle e_0|e^{-it(J+z\chi P_0)}|e_0\rangle$ in closed walks at $0$ of the loop-augmented path, where a step may be a hop ($\beta$), a loop at site $j\ge1$ ($\alpha_j$), or a loop at site $0$ ($z\chi$). Populations of a real symmetric matrix are even in $t$, so only even total degrees $d$ occur. A branch-odd term pairs two closed walks whose total number of $\chi$-loops is odd; the total number of remaining steps is then odd, so one of the two walks, after deleting its $\chi$-loops, is a closed walk at $0$ in $J$ of odd length. On a path every closed walk has an even number of hops, so an odd closed walk at $0$ uses an odd number of $\alpha$-loops; since $\alpha_1=\cdots=\alpha_{k-1}=0$ by the recursion of Item 1, it must reach site $k$, which costs $2k$ hops. Hence $d\ge(2k+1)+\#\{\chi\text{-loops}\}\ge2k+2$, and at $d=2k+2$ exactly one $\chi$-loop occurs, so the coefficient is linear in $\chi$.

For the value, let $u(t)=\langle e_0|e^{-itJ}|e_0\rangle=\sum_n(-it)^nm_n/n!$ and use Duhamel's formula to first order in $\chi$:
$$
 \langle e_0|e^{-it(J+z\chi P_0)}|e_0\rangle=u(t)-iz\chi\int_0^tu(t-s)u(s)\,ds+O(\chi^2),
$$
so that $\Delta M_{xx}(t)=4\chi\operatorname{Im}\!\big[\overline{u(t)}\int_0^tu(t-s)u(s)\,ds\big]+O(\chi^3)$, and the $O(\chi^3)$ part starts at degree $2k+4$ by the counting above. Split $u=u_e+u_o$ into the real even part $u_e=\sum_j(-1)^jt^{2j}m_{2j}/(2j)!$ and the imaginary odd part $u_o=-i\sum_{j\ge k}(-1)^jt^{2j+1}m_{2j+1}/(2j+1)!$. Only terms containing exactly one factor $u_o$ have a nonzero imaginary part, and $u_o=O(t^{2k+1})$. Keeping the lowest order, $u_e=1+O(t^2)$, $\int_0^tu_e(t-s)u_e(s)ds=t+O(t^3)$ and $\int_0^t[u_e(t-s)u_o(s)+u_o(t-s)u_e(s)]ds=2\int_0^tu_o(s)ds+O(t^{2k+4})$, one finds
$$
 \operatorname{Im}\!\big[\overline u\!\int\! u\,u\big]
 =(-1)^km_{2k+1}t^{2k+2}\Big[\frac{1}{(2k+1)!}-\frac{2}{(2k+2)!}\Big]+O(t^{2k+4})
 =\frac{2k\,(-1)^km_{2k+1}}{(2k+2)!}t^{2k+2}+O(t^{2k+4}).
$$
Multiplying by $4\chi$ gives (6.3).

Item 3. Odd rooted closed walks shorter than $\gamma_x$ do not exist, so $m_n=0$ for odd $n<\gamma_x$. If the minimal odd walks have weights of a common sign, $m_{\gamma_x}\ne0$, and (6.3) with $2k+1=\gamma_x$ is (6.1). □

Two consequences are worth stating. First, the local return probability decides the sign of a single-site potential exactly when the local density of states at that site is asymmetric; the criterion is spectral, not topological, and it applies to complex hopping. Second, when $\mu_x$ is symmetric the site can still be visible through transfer probabilities: for the real bow-tie of two unit-weight triangles sharing the site $0$, one of them carrying a single negative edge, all odd moments at $0$ vanish and the return contrast is identically zero for every $\chi$, although $\gamma_0=3$ and the support is unbalanced, while the transfer $0\to1$ has $[t^4]\Delta M_{10}=\chi/3$ by (7.1). Rows C11–C13 check (6.3) on a Jacobi path with $\alpha_1=0\ne\alpha_2$, on this bow-tie, and on a complex triangle with a pendant edge.

The symmetric case of Theorem 6.2 is not confined to bipartite supports or to real weights. The bow-tie is an instance of a general construction.

**Proposition 6.3 (odd symmetries and negated mirror gluing).** (a) If a unitary $W$ satisfies $WKW^\dagger=-K$ and $W|x\rangle=e^{i\theta}|x\rangle$, then every odd moment $m_n=(K^n)_{xx}$ vanishes, $\mu_x$ is symmetric, and $\chi P_x$ is invisible in the return probability at $x$ for every $\chi$. (b) Let $K_1$ be any zero-diagonal Hermitian matrix on a vertex set $X_1\ni x$, let $X_2$ be a disjoint copy of $X_1\setminus\{x\}$, and let $K=K_1\vee_x(-K_1)$ be the network on $X_1\cup X_2$ obtained by gluing a copy of $-K_1$ to $K_1$ at $x$. Then (a) applies with $W$ the permutation exchanging the two copies, so $\chi P_x$ is return-blind at $x$. If $K_1$ is unbalanced, so is $K$, and if some triangle $x,b,c$ of $K_1$ has $\sum_{c}\operatorname{Re}(K_{xb}K_{bc}K_{cx})\ne0$, the transfer $x\to b$ is visible at order $t^4$ by (7.1).

**Proof.** (a) $m_n=\langle x|K^n|x\rangle=\langle x|W^\dagger(WKW^\dagger)^nW|x\rangle=(-1)^n m_n$. Symmetry of $\mu_x$ and return blindness then follow from Theorem 6.2(1). (b) The copies share only $x$, so the permutation $W$ exchanging them fixes $|x\rangle$ and maps $K_1$ to $-K_1$ and back, that is $WKW^\dagger=-K$. $K_1$ is an induced sub-network of $K$, so an unbalanced cycle of $K_1$ is one of $K$. For the transfer, (7.1) with $V=P_x$, $a=x$ and $D=0$ gives $[t^4]\Delta M_{bx}=\tfrac13\sum_{c\ne x,b}\operatorname{Re}(\overline{K_{bx}}K_{bc}K_{cx})$, and only vertices $c$ of the copy containing $b$ contribute. □

Walk counting gives the same conclusion for (b): a closed walk at $x$ decomposes into excursions between consecutive visits to $x$, each excursion lies in one copy, and the mirror of an excursion of odd length carries the opposite sign, so odd-length walks cancel in pairs. Row C17 certifies (b) on a complex, unbalanced seven-site network: all odd moments at $x$ vanish, the return contrast for $\chi P_x$ has an exact zero certificate, and the transfer $x\to b$ is visible at $t^4$. The bipartite case is (a) with $W=\Sigma$ diagonal; the mirror construction shows that the diagonal choice is not essential and that return-blind sites exist on unbalanced supports with arbitrary complex weights, while the blindness remains local.

## 7. Low-order responses and reconstruction

### 7.1 General quartic response, including a nonconstant even diagonal

Let $H_z=K+D+zV$, with $K$ zero-diagonal Hermitian and $D,V$ real diagonal. For $a\ne b$,

$$
 [t^4]\Delta M_{ba}=
 -\frac{|K_{ba}|^2}{3}(d_a-d_b)(v_a-v_b)
 +\frac13\sum_{c\ne a,b}\operatorname{Re}(\overline{K_{ba}}K_{bc}K_{ca})(v_a+v_b-2v_c).
 \tag{7.1}
$$

The branch coefficients below order four vanish. The return coefficient is the negative sum of the off-diagonal coefficients in the same input column, by conservation of total probability.

For completeness, the fourth population coefficient is
$|(H_z^2)_{ba}|^2/4-\operatorname{Re}(\overline{K_{ba}}(H_z^3)_{ba})/3$. Write $e_a=d_a+zv_a$. Then

$$
 (H_z^2)_{ba}=(e_a+e_b)K_{ba}+\sum_{c\ne a,b}K_{bc}K_{ca},
$$
$$
 (H_z^3)_{ba}=(e_a^2+e_ae_b+e_b^2)K_{ba}
 +\sum_{c\ne a,b}(e_a+e_b+e_c)K_{bc}K_{ca}+(K^3)_{ba}.
$$

Subtracting $z=-1$ from $z=1$ gives (7.1). This calculation explains both the triangle term and the response on triangle-free graphs caused by $D$. For example, $K=\sigma_x$, $D=V=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$ gives coefficient $-1/3$, despite balanced hopping support. The zero/scalar-$D$ hypothesis in Theorems 4.1 and 5.1 cannot be silently dropped.

### 7.2 Two pulses and bipartite quadratures

For $U_z=e^{-i\tau(K_2+zV)}e^{-i\tau(K_1+zV)}$, the off-diagonal first amplitude coefficient is $-i(k_1+k_2)$. The part of the second coefficient odd in $z$ is

$$
 -\left[\frac{v_a+v_b}{2}(k_1+k_2)+k_2v_a+v_bk_1\right].
$$

Their probability cross term gives (5.1); terms without $V$ cancel between branches. Thus equal scalar offsets in $V$ cannot create a cubic signal.

As a useful specialization, take a real matrix $C$ and

$$
 X_C=\begin{pmatrix}0&C\\C^{\mathsf T}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 Y_C=\begin{pmatrix}0&-iC\\iC^{\mathsf T}&0\end{pmatrix}. \tag{7.2}
$$

Each mode is universally blind separately. On an edge directed from the first block to the second, two pulses give coefficient $4C_{ab}^2(v_a-v_b)$. On a connected support this is zero on every edge precisely when $V$ is scalar. Two pulses are sufficient and, since one is blind, minimal for any nonscalar $V$ in this family. The first possible time degree is three. This is the seed's exact quadrature result, now situated within Theorem 5.1.

If the oriented incidence matrix has rows $(Bv)_e=v_a-v_b$, the measured cubic vector is $c_3=4\operatorname{diag}(C_e^2)Bv$. Standard weighted least squares reconstructs $v$ modulo one constant per connected component. For connected support and measurement error $\epsilon$, the centered reconstruction obeys

$$
 \|\widehat v-v\|_2\le
 \frac{\|\epsilon\|_2}{4\sqrt{\lambda_2(B^{\mathsf T}\operatorname{diag}(C_e^4)B)}}.
 \tag{7.3}
$$

This pseudoinverse bound is standard linear algebra, not a separate novelty claim. It presumes known, nonzero edge weights and the same calibrated potential across the relevant experiments.


## 8. Fixed potentials: structure of the blind set and hidden blindness

Theorems 4.1 and 5.1 quantify over all diagonal potentials. For a particular $V$, unbalance is not sufficient for visibility. This section describes what can be said about the set of blind potentials of a fixed network, and exhibits a structured family in which a nonscalar potential is completely invisible although the gain graph is unbalanced.

### 8.1 The blind set is a proper algebraic set

For fixed $K$ let
$$
 \mathcal B(K)=\{V\text{ real diagonal}:\ \Delta M_{ba}(t;V)=0\ \text{for all }a,b,t\}.
$$

**Proposition 8.1.** $\mathcal B(K)$ is a real algebraic subset of $\mathbb R^N$, cut out by finitely many polynomials in $v$ of degree at most $2N(N-1)$ that are odd in $v$. It contains the scalar line and is invariant under $V\mapsto V+cI$ and $V\mapsto-V$. It equals $\mathbb R^N$ if and only if $K$ is balanced. If $K$ is unbalanced, $\mathcal B(K)$ is a proper algebraic set, hence Lebesgue-null and nowhere dense: every nonempty open set of potentials, including every neighbourhood of $0$, contains a witness.

**Proof.** By the finite derivative certificate of ZS-M74 [S1, Theorem F], $V\in\mathcal B(K)$ iff $\partial_t^k\Delta M_{ba}(0;V)=0$ for every $a,b$ and every $k=0,1,\ldots,2N(N-1)$; each derivative is a polynomial in $v$ of degree at most its order, and odd in $v$ because $\Delta M$ changes sign under $V\mapsto-V$. Scalar shifts are removable phases, and a proper real algebraic set has empty interior and measure zero. The dichotomy between $\mathbb R^N$ and a proper subset is Theorem 4.1. □

The exterior detuning of Section 4 is therefore a proof device for properness, not a resource requirement: once $K$ is unbalanced, almost every small potential is a witness. The finite polynomial description also follows the pattern of ZS-M74 [S1, Corollary T]; what is new here is the identification of the two alternatives with balance.

**Proposition 8.2 (line dichotomy).** For a fixed direction $V\ne0$, either $\chi V\in\mathcal B(K)$ for every $\chi\in\mathbb R$, or $\chi V$ is visible for all but finitely many $\chi$. In the first case the first-order response $\partial_\chi P_{ba}(K+\chi V)|_{\chi=0}$ vanishes identically in $t$ for all $a,b$.

**Proof.** $\{\chi:\chi V\in\mathcal B(K)\}$ is the common zero set in $\mathbb R$ of finitely many odd polynomials in $\chi$; it is either all of $\mathbb R$ or finite. If it is all of $\mathbb R$ then every coefficient polynomial vanishes identically, in particular its linear part. □

By Theorem 6.2, for $V=P_x$ the return probability alone realizes the second alternative with exceptional set exactly $\{0\}$ whenever $\mu_x$ is asymmetric; among nonzero strengths there is no exception.

### 8.2 Sublattice potentials on bipartite supports

Let the support of $K$ be bipartite with parts $A$ and $B$, and order the basis as $A$ then $B$, so that
$$
 K=\begin{pmatrix}0&B\\B^\dagger&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}I_A&0\\0&-I_B\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 V=\chi\Sigma+cI,\quad\chi\in\mathbb R .
 \tag{8.1}
$$
Here $B$ denotes the $|A|\times|B|$ coupling block $K_{AB}$. Put $G_A=BB^\dagger$, $G_B=B^\dagger B$, $\Lambda_A=(G_A+\chi^2)^{1/2}$, $\Lambda_B=(G_B+\chi^2)^{1/2}$, $C_X=\cos(t\Lambda_X)$, and $S_X=\sin(t\Lambda_X)/\Lambda_X$, the last being an entire function of $G_X+\chi^2$ and therefore well defined also when $\chi=0$.

**Theorem 8.3 (sublattice potentials).** With the notation (8.1), for every $t$:

1. $\Delta M(t)=M_{K+V}(t)-M_{K+V}(t)^{\mathsf T}$. Hence $V$ is blind if and only if $K+\chi\Sigma$ has time-symmetric populations in the sense $P_{ba}=P_{ab}$.
2. Every cross-sublattice population is blind: $\Delta M_{ba}=0$ whenever $a\in A,\ b\in B$ or $a\in B,\ b\in A$, for every $\chi$ and every complex $B$.
3. Within a part, $\Delta M_{a'a}=4\chi\operatorname{Im}\big(\overline{(C_A)_{a'a}}\,(S_A)_{a'a}\big)$ for $a,a'\in A$, and $\Delta M_{b'b}=-4\chi\operatorname{Im}\big(\overline{(C_B)_{b'b}}\,(S_B)_{b'b}\big)$ for $b,b'\in B$.
4. If $G_A$ is diagonally gauge-equivalent to a real symmetric matrix, or has at most two distinct eigenvalues, all within-$A$ populations are blind; in particular this holds whenever $|A|\le2$. The same applies to $B$.

**Corollary 8.4 (hidden blindness).** If $|A|\le2$ and $|B|\le2$, every sublattice potential $\chi\Sigma+cI$ is completely blind, for every complex coupling block. The only such connected support that can be unbalanced is the 4-cycle: a 4-cycle with arbitrary weights and arbitrary flux $\varphi\notin\{0,\pi\}$ is unbalanced, so $\mathcal B(K)$ strictly contains the scalar line, while Theorem 4.1 guarantees that some potential is visible; explicitly, label the cycle $0,1,2,3,0$, set $V=P_0$, and use the adjacent transition $1\to2$. The short arc avoids site $0$ and the complementary arc contains it, so Lemma 3.2 gives $4\operatorname{Re}\{(-i)^3h\}/4!$ at order $t^5$. The opposite transition $1\to3$ instead uses two arcs of length two and must use (3.2), not (3.3). For $K_{10}=i$, $K_{21}=K_{32}=K_{03}=1$ and Hermitian conjugate reverse edges, these coefficients are respectively $1/6$ and $-1/3$ (C20; see also C14, C19).

**Proof of Theorem 8.3.** (1) $\Sigma$ is a diagonal unitary, and $\Sigma K\Sigma=-K$ because $K$ has no entries within a part, while $\Sigma V\Sigma=V$. Hence $\Sigma(K+V)\Sigma=-(K-V)$, so $e^{-it(K-V)}=\Sigma e^{it(K+V)}\Sigma$, and $|e^{-it(K-V)}_{ba}|=|e^{it(K+V)}_{ba}|=|e^{-it(K+V)}_{ab}|$.

(2)–(3) Drop $c$. Since $K$ anticommutes with $\Sigma$, $H=K+\chi\Sigma$ satisfies $H^2=K^2+\chi^2=\Lambda_A^2\oplus\Lambda_B^2$, and $|H|=\Lambda_A\oplus\Lambda_B$ commutes with $H$. Therefore $e^{-itH}=\cos(t|H|)-i\,H\,\sin(t|H|)/|H|$, whose blocks are
$$
 U_{AA}=C_A-i\chi S_A,\qquad U_{BB}=C_B+i\chi S_B,\qquad U_{AB}=-iS_AB,\qquad U_{BA}=-iS_BB^\dagger .
$$
Because $B^\dagger G_A=G_BB^\dagger$, every function satisfies $f(G_B)B^\dagger=B^\dagger f(G_A)$, so $U_{BA}=-iB^\dagger S_A=-(U_{AB})^\dagger$. Hence $|(U_{BA})_{ba}|=|(U_{AB})_{ab}|$, which is (2). For (3), $C_A$ and $S_A$ are Hermitian, so $(C_A)_{aa'}=\overline{(C_A)_{a'a}}$ and likewise for $S_A$; writing $C=(C_A)_{a'a}$, $S=(S_A)_{a'a}$,
$$
 P_{a'a}-P_{aa'}=|C-i\chi S|^2-|\overline C-i\chi\overline S|^2=|C-i\chi S|^2-|C+i\chi S|^2=4\chi\operatorname{Im}(\overline CS).
$$
The sign is reversed on $B$ because $\Sigma=-I$ there.

(4) Let $G_A=\sum_j\lambda_jE_j$ over distinct eigenvalues. If $G_A=D R D^\dagger$ with $D$ diagonal unitary and $R$ real symmetric, then $E_j=DE_j(R)D^\dagger$ with $E_j(R)$ real, so $C_A$ and $S_A$ are $D(\text{real})D^\dagger$ and $\overline CS$ is real for every entry. If $G_A$ has at most two distinct eigenvalues then $E_2=I-E_1$, so for $a\ne a'$ the two projector entries are negatives of each other and again $\overline CS\in\mathbb R$. A $2\times2$ Hermitian matrix is diagonally gauge-equivalent to a real one, so $|A|\le2$ is covered. □

The sublattice sign converts the branch flip into time reversal, an instance of the earlier boost/sign-reversal mechanism [R15, Appendix A]. The two-eigenvalue clause in item (4) is the block form of [S1, §7.2], and Proposition 8.5 is a block specialization of the time-reversal formula of [S1, Theorem S]. These ingredients are credited. The cross-sublattice intertwining and its consequences for arbitrary complex coupling lead to the complete Gram criterion in Theorem 8.10; the sign-conversion identity alone is not claimed as a new principle.

The propagators of Corollary 8.4 with $\varphi\notin\{0,\pi\}$ and $\chi\ne0$ are time-symmetric although, for every sufficiently small $t>0$, no diagonal unitary $\Lambda$ satisfies $\Lambda U(t)\Lambda^\dagger=U(t)^{\mathsf T}$ or $=U(t)^\dagger$. The first relation would force $U_{ba}/U_{ab}=\Lambda_a/\Lambda_b$ on every edge, hence a product of these ratios equal to $1$ around the cycle, whereas $U_{ba}=-itK_{ba}+O(t^2)$ makes that product tend to the doubled gain $e^{2i\varphi}\ne1$ as $t\to0$; the second would force a real diagonal of $U$, whereas for $c=0$, $U_{aa}=(C_A)_{aa}-i\chi(S_A)_{aa}=1-i\chi t+O(t^2)$ on $A$. With arbitrary $c$, the diagonal generators are $c+\chi$ and $c-\chi$; at least one is nonzero, which gives the same obstruction. At $\chi=0,c=0$, the relation $\Sigma U\Sigma=U^\dagger$ does hold, so that boundary is excluded. On the tested time grid both obstructions persist at every point except isolated ones (row V07), so the failure is not a revival-time artefact. The small-time conclusion follows by continuity of the nonunit cycle ratio and the nonzero diagonal derivative, not from the finite time grid. The family persists under changes of its edge weights and flux within the staggered-potential constraint; it is not claimed to persist under arbitrary diagonal perturbations. This obstructs extending the diagonal-gauge necessity sentence of [R2] to arbitrary on-site terms. The Methods paragraph of [R2, v2 p. 7] explicitly fixes the Hamiltonian diagonal to zero; the present $\chi\ne0$ quadrilateral lies outside that premise. The already-recorded zero-diagonal $K_4$ example [S1, §7.1] is the relevant counterexample within that premise, as detailed in §8.6. Neither example changes the validity of the gauge-real or zero-diagonal bipartite sufficient theorems. Row C14 gives an exact certificate for the flux 4-cycle, row C19 exact certificates for random complex weights with a scalar shift, row V05 the $\Lambda$ obstruction, and row C15 shows the boundary of the criterion: on $K_{2,3}$ the within-$A$ and cross populations are blind while a within-$B$ population is visible, and on a flux 6-cycle the cross populations are blind while the whole potential is visible.

**Proposition 8.5 (spectral formula at nonzero strength).** Fix $\chi\ne0$. Let $\nu_1<\cdots<\nu_p$ be the distinct eigenvalues of $\Lambda_A$ and $e_j=(E_j)_{a'a}$ the corresponding eigenprojector entries of $G_A$. Then
$$
 \Delta M_{a'a}(t)=2\chi\sum_{j<k}\operatorname{Im}(\overline{e_j}e_k)\Big[\Big(\frac1{\nu_k}-\frac1{\nu_j}\Big)\sin((\nu_j+\nu_k)t)+\Big(\frac1{\nu_k}+\frac1{\nu_j}\Big)\sin((\nu_k-\nu_j)t)\Big],
 \tag{8.2}
$$
so the within-$A$ populations are blind for all $t$ if and only if, for every $\omega>0$ and every pair $a,a'$, the total coefficient of $\sin(\omega t)$ obtained by merging equal frequencies $\nu_j+\nu_k$ and $\nu_k-\nu_j$ vanishes. The non-resonant special case gives entrywise phase alignment immediately. Theorem 8.10 below proves that the same equivalence holds even when the sums and differences coincide. At $\chi=0$, blindness is automatic; (8.2) is interpreted only by its continuous limit if a $\nu_j$ is zero.

**Proof.** Insert $C=\sum_j\cos(\nu_jt)e_j$ and $S=\sum_j\sin(\nu_jt)e_j/\nu_j$ into item 3 and use $\cos\alpha\sin\beta=\tfrac12[\sin(\alpha+\beta)+\sin(\beta-\alpha)]$. Finitely many sines with distinct positive frequencies are linearly independent. □

Row V06 checks (8.2)-type vanishing and non-vanishing on a $|A|=3$ network. Phase alignment holds for gauge-real $G_A$ and for two-eigenvalue $G_A$ but is not equivalent to gauge reality: the $K_4$ matrix of [S1, §7.1], shifted by $\sqrt3\,I$ to make it positive semidefinite, has two eigenvalues and is therefore phase-aligned, while its triangle products are imaginary, so it is not gauge-real.

### 8.3 Minimality and larger hidden-blind supports

**Proposition 8.6 (minimality).** If $N\le3$ and $K$ is unbalanced, then $\mathcal B(K)$ is the scalar line: every nonscalar $V$ is visible, already at order $t^4$. Consequently the flux 4-cycle of Corollary 8.4 is, by vertex count, the smallest unbalanced network that carries a completely blind nonscalar potential.

**Proof.** For $N\le2$ there is no cycle and every $K$ is balanced. For $N=3$, unbalance requires the full triangle with $\operatorname{Re}h\ne0$, where $h=K_{10}K_{21}K_{02}$; reversing the orientation conjugates $h$ and leaves $\operatorname{Re}h$ unchanged. By (7.1) with $D=0$, for each transition $a\to b$ with third vertex $c$,
$$
 [t^4]\Delta M_{ba}=\tfrac13\operatorname{Re}(h)\,(v_a+v_b-2v_c).
$$
If all three coefficients vanish then $v_0+v_1=2v_2$, $v_1+v_2=2v_0$, $v_0+v_2=2v_1$, whose only solutions are $v_0=v_1=v_2$. □

Row C16 verifies the three coefficients symbolically for complex weights and the forced scalar solution. The statement is sharp in the sense of vertex count only; an unbalanced network on four vertices other than the 4-cycle may or may not carry a hidden blind potential, and no claim is made.

**Proposition 8.7 (unitary coupling blocks).** Let $B$ be an $n\times n$ block with $BB^\dagger=\lambda I$, $\lambda>0$, for instance a complex Hadamard or the Fourier matrix $F_{ab}=\omega^{ab}/\sqrt n$, $\omega=e^{2\pi i/n}$. Then $K=\begin{pmatrix}0&B\\B^\dagger&0\end{pmatrix}$ on its actual support satisfies $K^2=\lambda I$, and every sublattice potential $\chi\Sigma+cI$ is completely blind. If every entry of $B$ is nonzero, the support is $K_{n,n}$ and is unbalanced iff some 4-cycle product $B_{ab}\overline{B_{a'b}}B_{a'b'}\overline{B_{ab'}}$ is non-real; for the Fourier matrix this product is $\omega^{(a-a')(b-b')}/n^2$, non-real for $n\ge3$.

**Proof.** $G_A=G_B=\lambda I$ have one eigenvalue, so Theorem 8.3(4) applies to both parts, and (2) covers the cross entries. On complete bipartite support every cycle product is generated by the displayed 4-cycle products, and $\omega^{(a-a')(b-b')}$ with $a-a'=b-b'=1$ is $\omega\notin\mathbb R$ for $n\ge3$. □

In this family $H=K+\chi\Sigma$ has the two eigenvalues $\pm\sqrt{\lambda+\chi^2}$, so its time symmetry is the two-eigenvalue mechanism of [S1, §7.2] applied to the sublattice family; hidden blindness of a nonscalar potential therefore occurs on unbalanced supports of every size $2n\ge6$. Row C18 gives an exact certificate for the Fourier $K_{3,3}$. A unitary block need not be dense: $B=I_3$ has only three disjoint edges, not $K_{3,3}$ (W06); its blindness still follows, but the full-support cycle assertion is not applied to it. The 4-cycle family of Corollary 8.4 is different in kind: it has four distinct eigenvalues in general (Proposition 8.8).

### 8.4 Single-site potentials

For $V=\chi P_x$ with $\chi\ne0$ on a connected unbalanced support, Theorem 6.2 shows visibility through the return at $x$ whenever $\mu_x$ is asymmetric, and Proposition 8.2 shows that visibility of the line $\mathbb R P_x$ is decided by finitely many polynomial identities. Proposition 6.3 shows that return-blind sites exist on unbalanced supports with arbitrary complex weights, but in those examples the transfers remain visible. Theorem 8.9 settles the conjecture affirmatively on every unbalanced bare cycle. Whether a nonzero single-site potential can be completely blind on an arbitrary connected unbalanced support is not settled here. A numerical time-grid search over signed $K_4$ signings, complete graphs with random phases, odd and even cycles with flux, the cube, $K_{3,3}$, the Petersen graph with random phases, and random Hermitian matrices up to $N=6$ found no blind single site, and the bow-tie example of Section 6.2 shows that return blindness does not extend to transfers. We record the statement "every nonzero single-site potential is visible on a connected unbalanced support" as a conjecture supported by finite evidence only; an accidental population symmetry of $K+\chi P_x$ of the kind exhibited by the sublattice family would be the way to refute it.

### 8.5 Finite certificates for specified pairs

A general finite test for specified matrices is available from standard observable-space methods [R6]. Define on pairs of Hermitian matrices
$$
 \mathcal G_j=\mathcal L_{+,j}\oplus\mathcal L_{-,j},\qquad
 \mathcal L_{z,j}(X)=-i[H_{z,j},X],\qquad x_a=(P_a,P_a),
$$
and $\ell_b(X,Y)=\operatorname{Tr}P_b(X-Y)$. Generate the real span of all $\mathcal G_{j_k}\cdots\mathcal G_{j_1}x_a$. Blindness for that input and all switching experiments is equivalent to $\ell_b$ vanishing on this span for every $b$. The equivalence follows from the multivariate Taylor expansion; a nonzero coefficient cannot vanish on the full open positive-duration domain. A stabilized span is invariant and the procedure stops, with ambient dimension at most $2N^2$. The safe word-length bound $2N^2-1$ is sufficient for one initial vector, not generally optimal. Repeat for each input configuration to certify all-population blindness. For a single static pair the merged-gap certificate of [S1, Theorem F] with $2N(N-1)$ derivatives is the one used in rows C12, C14, C15 and W04.

With unit edges $01,02,12,03$ and edge $13=-1$, the fourth coefficient for $0\to1$ is zero under $V=P_0$ and $-2/3$ under $V=P_2$: a cancellation at one readout, which does not establish blindness of that potential. Corollary 8.4 shows that complete blindness of a nonscalar potential on an unbalanced support does occur. The graph classification of Sections 4–5 therefore answers the all-$V$ question only; the fixed-potential question is spectral in the local case (Theorem 6.2), symmetry-driven in the sublattice case (Theorem 8.3), and algebraic in general (Proposition 8.1 and the certificates above). None of this supplies an action-derived specification of allowed controls.

### 8.6 Precise comparison with time-symmetric walks

Gauge-real Hamiltonians with real diagonal terms and zero-diagonal Hamiltonians on bipartite supports are established sufficient families [R2, v2 Proposition 5 and Theorem 4; R10, §3; R11, Methods]. Lu et al. also give the even/odd-propagator condition [R10, Eq. (1)]. Their geometric discussion identifies sufficient classes and potential asymmetry outside them; it is not an exhaustive classification of Hamiltonians with specially constrained weights. Accordingly, the present sublattice family extends the displayed sufficient examples, but does not refute a no-go theorem against all on-site terms.

Turner–Biamonte additionally state a diagonal-gauge necessity criterion for time-symmetric propagators [R2, v2 p. 4]. Their Methods argument on p. 7 explicitly fixes the Hamiltonian diagonal to zero. The nonzero-strength quadrilateral of §8.2 is therefore an obstruction to an arbitrary-diagonal extension, not a counterexample satisfying that zero-diagonal premise.

Within the zero-diagonal premise, the existing matrix $H=iA$ of [S1, §7.1], with $A$ real skew-symmetric, full $K_4$ support, and $A^2=-3I$, gives $U(t)=\cos(\sqrt3t)I+\sin(\sqrt3t)A/\sqrt3$. Its entry moduli are symmetric. At non-revival times, a diagonal conjugation to either $U^{\mathsf T}$ or $U^\dagger$ would require edge phase ratios $-1$ around a triangle, whose product is $-1$ rather than $1$. W07 rechecks this imported example; it is not a new result of this release. A switching-equivalent matrix already appears in [R16, §2, Fig. 2], which is credited as an external matrix source. The inference from equal populations to the completely positive, product-preserving map postulated in [R2] is not supplied by equality of the measured entries alone. This comparison leaves the gauge-real and zero-diagonal bipartite sufficient theorems untouched, and is separate from the novelty of Theorems 8.9–8.10.

The sign-conversion step itself is related to the earlier bipartite boost identity for opposite Hubbard interactions [R15, Appendix A, Eqs. (A1)–(A3)]. Here the additional classification concerns arbitrary complex rectangular coupling blocks with flux, all labelled configuration transitions, and exactly staggered on-site terms. Theorem 8.10 decides the full sublattice family by finite Gram-power data; Corollary 8.11 extracts a leading witness without matching resonant frequencies. The precise advance is this complete criterion and response law, not a claim that time symmetry with on-site terms was previously unknown outside gauge-real models.

The mechanism differs from the $K_4$ exception. Let $\lambda_1<\cdots<\lambda_p$ be the distinct positive eigenvalues of $G_A$; they are also eigenvalues of $G_B$, since $BB^\dagger$ and $B^\dagger B$ share their nonzero spectrum.

**Proposition 8.8 (paired spectrum and forced resonance).** For $H=K+\chi\Sigma$ with $\chi\ne0$: (a) for each $\lambda_j$ and each eigenvector $u$ of $G_A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_j$, the plane spanned by $(u,0)$ and $(0,B^\dagger u)$ is $H$-invariant and carries the eigenvalues $\pm\nu_j$, $\nu_j=\sqrt{\lambda_j+\chi^2}$; the kernels of $B^\dagger$ and $B$ carry the eigenvalues $+\chi$ and $-\chi$. Hence the spectrum of $H$ consists of $\pm\nu_1,\ldots,\pm\nu_p$ together with $+\chi$ and $-\chi$ of multiplicities $\dim\ker B^\dagger$ and $\dim\ker B$, and whenever $p\ge2$ every difference $\nu_k-\nu_j$ of two positive eigenvalues is also the difference $(-\nu_j)-(-\nu_k)$, so the positive Bohr gaps of $H$ are not pairwise distinct. (b) Consequently the non-resonance lemma of [S1, §7.2] (distinct gaps and a full-support rank-one eigenprojector imply that time symmetry forces gauge reality) never applies to a member with $p\ge2$. For a completely blind member the merged coefficient of $\sin\omega t$ in [S1, Theorem S] vanishes at every $\omega>0$; when the forced coincidences are the only ones among the positive gaps, this means that the per-frequency coefficients vanish individually at the frequencies $2\nu_j$ and cancel between the partner pairs $(-\nu_k,-\nu_j)$ and $(\nu_j,\nu_k)$ at the frequencies $\nu_k-\nu_j$.

**Proof.** (a) With $H=\begin{pmatrix}\chi I&B\\B^\dagger&-\chi I\end{pmatrix}$, $H(u,0)=(\chi u,B^\dagger u)$ and $H(0,B^\dagger u)=(BB^\dagger u,-\chi B^\dagger u)=(\lambda_ju,-\chi B^\dagger u)$, so the plane is invariant with matrix $\begin{pmatrix}\chi&\lambda_j\\1&-\chi\end{pmatrix}$ in this basis, of trace $0$ and determinant $-\chi^2-\lambda_j$; its eigenvalues are $\pm\nu_j$. A vector $(u,0)$ with $B^\dagger u=0$ is an eigenvector for $+\chi$, and $(0,v)$ with $Bv=0$ for $-\chi$. These planes and kernels span the space. The gap statement is immediate. (b) The lemma's hypothesis fails by (a). For a completely blind member, $M_H=M_H^{\mathsf T}$ for all $t$ by Theorem 8.3(1), so by [S1, Theorem S] the merged coefficient of $\sin\omega t$ vanishes for every $\omega>0$; if no further coincidence occurs, only the pair $(-\nu_j,\nu_j)$ contributes at $\omega=2\nu_j$, and exactly the two partner pairs contribute at $\omega=\nu_k-\nu_j$. □

Row V07 exhibits the 4-cycle family with four distinct eigenvalues, eigenprojectors without zero entries, individual partner terms of size $3\times10^{-2}$ and merged coefficients at the $10^{-17}$ level. The escape from the gauge criterion is thus a forced resonance of the $\pm\nu$ pairing, present for every flux and every choice of weights, not an accidental degeneracy; the $K_4$ example, by contrast, has only two eigenvalues, so all of its Bohr gaps coincide at a single frequency and the cancellation there is total. No exhaustion of all probability-time-symmetric Hamiltonians is claimed. Theorem 8.10 is complete within the staggered bipartite family; [S1, Theorem S] remains a general fixed-Hamiltonian test.

### 8.7 Complete fixed-potential classification on a bare cycle

The existence of a hidden-blind plane on the 4-cycle raises a sharper question: are there further blind potentials, and does the same mechanism persist on longer cycles? The answer is exact for every fixed set of nonzero complex edge weights.

**Theorem 8.9 (bare-cycle classification and sharp witness order).** Let the support of the zero-diagonal Hermitian matrix $K$ be exactly the cycle $C_\ell$, $\ell\ge3$, with every cycle edge nonzero, and let $\mathcal B(K)$ be the all-population blind set of Section 8.1.

1. If the transformed gain cycle is balanced, $\mathcal B(K)=\mathbb R^\ell$.
2. If it is unbalanced and $\ell=4$, with cyclic vertex order $0,1,2,3$, then
$$
 \mathcal B(K)=\{\operatorname{diag}(\alpha,\beta,\alpha,\beta):\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb R\}
 =\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\{I,\Sigma\}. \tag{8.3}
$$
3. If it is unbalanced and $\ell\ne4$, $\mathcal B(K)=\mathbb RI$.
4. In either unbalanced case, every $V\notin\mathcal B(K)$ has some transition whose first nonzero branch-contrast coefficient is exactly at time degree $\ell+1$.

**Proof.** The balanced case follows from Theorem 4.1. In the unbalanced case, orient the cycle and take the two complementary arcs between any distinct endpoints, with lengths $k$ and $\ell-k$ and potential sums $S_1,S_2$. In (3.2), the factor multiplying its real bracket is nonzero: for odd $\ell$ it selects the nonzero real part of the cycle product, and for even $\ell$ its nonzero imaginary part. Therefore the coefficient vanishes precisely when
$$
 (k+1)S_2=(\ell-k+1)S_1. \tag{8.4}
$$
This is equality of the two vertex-average potentials along the arcs; endpoints are counted once in each arc.

Put $T=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}v_j$. For adjacent endpoints $j,j+1$, choose $k=1$. Then $S_1=v_j+v_{j+1}$ and $S_2=T$, so (8.4) gives
$$
 v_j+v_{j+1}=2T/\ell \quad\text{for all }j. \tag{8.5}
$$
Subtract consecutive equations to obtain $v_{j+2}=v_j$. An odd cycle consequently forces all potentials to be equal. On an even cycle $\ell=2m$, the only remaining possibility is an alternating pair of values $\alpha,\beta$.

Choose a two-edge arc starting on the $\alpha$ sublattice. Its sum is $2\alpha+\beta$, while the complementary arc has sum $m\alpha+(m-1)\beta$. Equation (8.4) becomes
$$
 3\{m\alpha+(m-1)\beta\}-(2m-1)(2\alpha+\beta)
 =(m-2)(\beta-\alpha)=0. \tag{8.6}
$$
For $m\ge3$ this forces $\alpha=\beta$. For $m=2$, every alternating potential is blind by Corollary 8.4, for arbitrary weights. Scalar potentials are always blind. Thus the kernel of the leading-coefficient map is precisely the asserted blind set. The girth barrier excludes all smaller degrees, proving item 4. $\square$

The exceptional role of the quadrilateral is therefore a classification, not just an example. Unbalanced $C_6,C_8,\ldots$ do not retain the hidden staggered-potential line. A nonzero single-site potential is visible on every unbalanced bare cycle, including $C_4$, although the corresponding statement on arbitrary connected supports remains open. Rows C21–C23 compare the linear kernels and weighted coefficients using exact arithmetic and an amplitude-series implementation distinct from the inherited density-series route. The theorem is proved for all $\ell$ above; the finite checks do not replace that proof.

### 8.8 A complete sublattice criterion without a non-resonance hypothesis

The merged-frequency formula (8.2) remains valid, but its apparent dependence on spectral resonances can be eliminated in the sublattice family. The missing relation is that its two scalar functions are a function and its derivative.

For a Hermitian $m\times m$ matrix $G=\sum_{j=1}^p\lambda_jE_j$, say that its spectral projectors are **entrywise phase-aligned** if
$$
 \operatorname{Im}\!\left(\overline{(E_j)_{ba}}(E_k)_{ba}\right)=0
 \quad\text{for every }a,b,j,k. \tag{8.7}
$$
For each fixed entry, this means that all projector entries lie on one real line in $\mathbb C$, allowing zero entries. The line may depend on $(b,a)$; it need not arise from one diagonal gauge for the whole matrix.

**Theorem 8.10 (complete Gram criterion; nonzero-strength independence).** In (8.1), fix any $\chi\ne0$. The following are equivalent for the $A$ part:

1. Every within-$A$ population is blind for all time at this fixed $\chi$.
2. The spectral projectors of $G_A=BB^\dagger$ are entrywise phase-aligned.
3. For $m=|A|$,
$$
 \operatorname{Im}\!\left(\overline{(G_A^r)_{a'a}}(G_A^s)_{a'a}\right)=0
 \quad\text{for every }a,a'\in A,\quad0\le r,s\le m-1. \tag{8.8}
$$

The identical equivalence holds for $G_B=B^\dagger B$. Consequently complete blindness at one nonzero $\chi$ is equivalent to complete blindness at every real $\chi$, and is decided by (8.8) on both parts. If one Gram block fails this criterion, the staggered potential is visible for **every** nonzero $\chi$. At $\chi=0$ the two branches coincide and are always blind, independently of (8.7). Rectangular coupling blocks, disconnected supports, zero singular values and repeated eigenvalues are allowed; projectors are always taken over distinct eigenvalues.

**Proof.** Fix one entry and suppress its indices. Let
$$
 f(t)=\left[\frac{\sin(t\sqrt{G_A+\chi^2I})}{\sqrt{G_A+\chi^2I}}\right]_{a'a},
 \qquad f'(t)=[\cos(t\sqrt{G_A+\chi^2I})]_{a'a}.
 \tag{8.9}
$$
Both are entire in $t$. Writing $f=u+iv$ with real analytic $u,v$, Theorem 8.3 gives
$$
 \Delta M_{a'a}(t)=4\chi\operatorname{Im}(\overline{f'(t)}f(t))
 =4\chi\{u'(t)v(t)-v'(t)u(t)\}. \tag{8.10}
$$
If this vanishes identically and $f$ is not identically zero, multiply $f$ by a constant phase if necessary so that $u$ is not identically zero. On an interval where $u\ne0$, $(v/u)'=0$. Hence $v=du$ there for a real constant $d$, and real analyticity extends this identity to all real $t$. Thus $f(t)$ has a constant complex phase up to a real factor. If $f\equiv0$, the same conclusion holds with every coefficient zero.

The expansion
$$
 f(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}(E_j)_{a'a}\frac{\sin(\nu_jt)}{\nu_j},
 \qquad \nu_j=\sqrt{\lambda_j+\chi^2}>0, \tag{8.11}
$$
has distinct positive $\nu_j$. The scalar functions in (8.11) are linearly independent over $\mathbb C$: their odd derivatives at zero give the Vandermonde matrix in the distinct numbers $\nu_j^2$. Constant phase of $f$ therefore forces every $(E_j)_{a'a}$ to have that same phase up to a real sign. The converse follows by substituting phase-aligned coefficients into (8.9)–(8.10). This proves 1$\Leftrightarrow$2 without separating sums or differences of frequencies.

For 2$\Rightarrow$3, powers of $G_A$ are real linear combinations of its projectors. Conversely each projector is a polynomial in $G_A$ of degree at most $p-1\le m-1$ with real coefficients:
$$
 E_j=\prod_{k\ne j}\frac{G_A-\lambda_kI}{\lambda_j-\lambda_k}.
$$
For each entry, (8.8) places all power entries on one real line, so it places the projector entries there as well. This proves 3$\Rightarrow$2. The $B$-part argument changes only the overall sign in (8.10). Cross-sublattice populations are always blind by Theorem 8.3(2); combining the two parts proves the complete statement. $\square$

This supplies a finite test using only powers of the two Gram matrices, with no eigenvalue-gap matching and no search over $\chi$. The criterion is stronger than the sufficient cases in Theorem 8.3(4). It does not assert gauge reality: if $A$ is the real skew-conference matrix in [S1, §7.1] and $B=2I+iA$, then $G_A=G_B=7I+4iA$ has the quadratic relation $G^2-14G+I=0$. Its projectors are phase-aligned, yet its non-real triangle product prevents a diagonal real gauge (C26, V10). Theorem 8.10 also does not extend the same criterion to arbitrary diagonal $V$.

**Corollary 8.11 (sharp within-part response from Gram powers).** Fix distinct $a,a'\in A$ and write $g_k=(G_A^k)_{a'a}$. If all $g_k$ vanish or are phase-aligned, this entry is blind for every $\chi$. Otherwise let
$$
 r=\min\{k\ge1:g_k\ne0\},\qquad
 s=\min\{k>r:\operatorname{Im}(\overline{g_r}g_k)\ne0\}.
$$
For every $\chi\ne0$, its first visible time degree is $q=2(r+s)+1$, with coefficient
$$
 [t^q]\Delta M_{a'a}
 =\frac{8\chi(r-s)(-1)^{r+s}}{(2r+1)!(2s+1)!}
 \operatorname{Im}(\overline{g_r}g_s). \tag{8.12}
$$
For the $B$ part the sign is reversed. If $p$ is the number of distinct eigenvalues of the relevant Gram matrix, the nonaligned case has $1\le r<s\le p-1$, hence $q\le4p-5\le4m-5$. For $r=1,s=2$, (8.12) reduces to
$$
 [t^7]\Delta M_{a'a}
 =\frac{\chi}{90}\operatorname{Im}\!\left(\overline{(G_A)_{a'a}}(G_A^2)_{a'a}\right).
 \tag{8.13}
$$

**Proof.** In the Taylor expansion $f(t)=\sum_{k\ge0}a_kt^{2k+1}$ of (8.9),
$$
 a_k=\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)!}[(G_A+\chi^2I)^k]_{a'a}.
$$
Because $a\ne a'$, $g_0=0$. The shift $G_A\mapsto G_A+\chi^2I$ preserves the first nonzero power $r$ and the first nonaligned power $s$: by the binomial formula, lower powers before $r$ vanish, and the intervening powers before $s$ lie on the real line of $g_r$. In particular the first nonzero imaginary product remains $\operatorname{Im}(\overline{g_r}g_s)$. Pairing indices $k<l$ in $\operatorname{Im}(\overline{f'}f)$ gives coefficient $2(k-l)\operatorname{Im}(\overline{a_k}a_l)$ at degree $2(k+l)+1$. All smaller degrees vanish, and at the first degree the only nonzero pair is $(r,s)$. Multiplication by $4\chi$ gives (8.12). Real polynomial interpolation on the $p$ eigenvalues spans all powers by $I,G_A,\ldots,G_A^{p-1}$, so any first nonaligned pair occurs within that range. $\square$

For an entry with $\operatorname{Im}(\overline{G_{a'a}}(G^2)_{a'a})\ne0$, the first response within a part is exactly of degree seven. If this condition fails, the response may occur later or vanish identically, with (8.12) deciding the nonaligned case. No generic visibility assertion is made across all block sizes or rank constraints; in particular a part of size at most two is always blind. C24 certifies a symbolic $\chi$-linear seventh-order example, C25 an eleventh-order example with $r=2,s=3$, and V08 deliberately includes coincident sum/difference frequencies and a singular Gram matrix. The vanishing Wronskian is the reason these resonances cannot create an exceptional nonzero strength in the sublattice family. Forced gap resonances of the full Hamiltonian in Proposition 8.8 still occur and remain compatible with this stronger criterion.


## 9. Conditional connection to Z-Spin models

The mathematical result addresses a specified pair or family of finite generators and specified preparations/readouts. Its role in the project is a support method for sign-sensitive records. It does not select a subsystem, a physical clock, an initial environment, an instrument, or a single-event rule.

The pointwise normalized Pauli frame in ZS-M69 Appendix B.3 [S3] is

$$
 X_\theta=-\sin\theta\,\sigma_x+\cos\theta\,\sigma_y,\quad
 Y_\theta=\cos\theta\,\sigma_x+\sin\theta\,\sigma_y,\quad
 J_E=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1).
$$

For the lower off-diagonal entries, $\operatorname{Im}(\overline{k_X}k_Y)=-1$. For putative modes $\gamma_1X_\theta,\gamma_2Y_\theta$, real calibrated amplitudes $\gamma_1,\gamma_2$, and $V=MJ_E$, (5.1) gives

$$
 [\tau^3]\Delta M_{10}=8\gamma_1\gamma_2M,\qquad M=m\sin(\theta+\theta_m). \tag{9.1}
$$

The normalized choice $\gamma_1=\gamma_2=1$ reproduces the seed's $8M$. This is a pointwise algebraic candidate test. It is not a completed test of two action-derived physical transports. The needed moving-subspace transport, leakage bounds, mode selection, normalization of physical time, preparation, and readout remain unspecified. At $M=0$ or a zero mode amplitude the displayed witness vanishes.

Similarly, a static bosonic Hessian is not automatically the one-excitation quantum generator. ZS-M54 [S4] distinguishes its static $K_{2,9}$ weight from quantum coupling normalization. A branch-odd hopping block of the form

$$
 \begin{pmatrix}D_A&zB\\zB^\dagger&D_B\end{pmatrix}
$$

has a common diagonal unitary switching symmetry and is population blind even under switching when that same symmetry persists; it is a different placement of the branch sign from (2.1). Finally, the affine $\Phi_Z$-linear Yukawa slot discussed in ZS-S14 [S5] has zero direct bosonic Hessian when other slots are fixed. That local derivative identity neither supplies a nonzero onsite $V$ nor proves a no-go for all possible effective actions.

The sublattice potential $\chi\Sigma$ of Section 8.2 is the many-site analogue of the two-site $MJ_E$: on a bipartite carrier with parts of size at most two, such a potential is invisible to every single-mode population experiment, whatever the flux, so a sign-sensitive record in that geometry must use either mode switching (Theorem 5.1, (9.1)) or a non-sublattice potential. This is a statement about the finite matrix model only.

Accordingly, the outstanding action-to-instrument and event-selection debts remain open. Neither a gain graph nor its holonomy is identified here with a physical Z-Spin carrier, a Born rule, an i-tetration phase, or a geometrical time increment.

The principal limitations are precise. The results are finite-dimensional. The universal classification quantifies over all real diagonal potentials and all configuration inputs/outputs and assumes zero or removable scalar branch-even diagonals. The multimode theorem permits finite coherent switching with positive durations but imposes no fixed hardware time-resolution limit. The sublattice criterion is complete for arbitrary coupling blocks by Theorem 8.10 and requires Gram entries, not only the support graph. Minimality of the 4-cycle is a vertex-count statement. Single-site complete blindness on arbitrary connected unbalanced supports remains open beyond the cases proved here. No claim is made that the classes of Section 8.6 exhaust time-symmetric walks. Actual physical resources require a separate derivation.

## 11. Discussion

For fixed hopping matrices, requiring invisibility under every diagonal sign test removes the exceptional cancellations that obstruct a simple classification of one fixed pair. The obstruction is exactly the balance of a transformed gain graph. Exterior detuning makes this necessity constructive: an unbalanced induced cycle cannot be hidden by fixed couplings to the rest of the network. Positive mixing then extends the classification to a common gauge for coherent mode switching.

Dropping the universal quantifier changes the answer. The local return probability is governed by the local spectral measure. For a bare unbalanced cycle, all blind potentials are scalar except for the exactly classified alternating family on the quadrilateral. In the staggered bipartite family, cross-sublattice blindness is universal but complete blindness is equivalent to phase alignment of the two Gram algebras. The identity $S'=C$ converts a resonant sine-cancellation problem into a constant-phase problem and removes the apparent exceptional nonzero strengths. The first nonaligned Gram powers also determine the sharp response order and its coefficient. These classifications concern declared finite generators and configuration measurements; selecting and physically realizing those inputs remains a separate problem.
